# Narrative insight

Turn numbers into a decision. The audience is a credit risk executive who has
four minutes and will be asked to approve something.

## Structure

Write in this order. It is the order the reader needs, not the order the
analysis happened in.

**1. The headline (one sentence).**
The single most decision-relevant fact, with its number.
> *$2.8B of mortgage balances reprice before the end of 2027 at an average
> payment increase of 31%.*

**2. So what (two or three sentences).**
Why it matters in money or risk terms. Quantify. If you cannot attach a dollar
figure or a rate to a claim, it is probably not worth the reader's attention.

**3. Where it concentrates.**
The segments that carry disproportionate exposure. A short table beats a
paragraph. Name the dimension that discriminates most, not every dimension you
have.

**4. Recommendation.**
Specific, owned, and sized. "Target proactive contact at the 2,013 accounts in
the severe shock band ahead of their renewal date, prioritising the $0.35B in
Ontario" — not "consider monitoring the portfolio".

**5. What would change this conclusion.**
State the load-bearing assumptions and their direction of effect. This is the
section that distinguishes an analyst from a report generator, and it is the
first thing a good executive asks for.

## Rules

- **Quantify or cut.** Every claim carries a number, or it does not appear.
- **Never state a figure the query did not return.** If you want to say
  something you have not measured, measure it or drop it. This is the single
  most common way an analyst loses credibility.
- **Distinguish correlation from mechanism.** Say which one you have. Where the
  data shows a relationship the model *built in*, say so explicitly rather than
  presenting it as a discovery.
- **Flag the simulated layer.** Loan-level data is simulated; macro data may be
  live or scenario. Any forward-looking figure inherits that caveat. State it
  once, clearly, rather than hedging every sentence.
- **Lead with the exception.** Executives read for what is unusual. If severe
  payment shock is concentrated in *prime* borrowers rather than subprime — as
  it is in this portfolio — that inversion is the story, because it is what
  makes conventional credit-score-based early warning miss it.

## Length

Four hundred words maximum for a monthly commentary. If it runs longer, the
analysis is not finished.
